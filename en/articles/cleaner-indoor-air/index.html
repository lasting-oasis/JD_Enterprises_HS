Cleaner air at home.

In a home with central heating and cooling, most of the air moves through that system. Three things matter most: the filter, the humidity and where the pollution comes from.

Start with the filter

Whenever the system's fan runs, the air passes through its filter. The EPA's advice: if you upgrade, choose a filter rated at least MERV 13, or as high as your system's fan and filter slot can handle. A technician can tell you which ratings and sizes fit.

Whatever the rating, a clogged filter slows the airflow. ENERGY STAR recommends checking it every month and changing it at least every three months.

Keep humidity in range

Damp air feeds mold, and very dry air is uncomfortable. The EPA's guidance is to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, and ideally between 30 and 50 percent. An inexpensive humidity meter shows where your home is.

  • Too humid: an air conditioner removes moisture as it cools. If rooms still feel clammy, a whole-home dehumidifier can help.
  • Too dry in winter: a whole-home humidifier adds moisture back.
  • Leaks and spills: the EPA says drying wet materials in 24 to 48 hours usually keeps mold from growing.

Deal with the sources

Filters and air cleaners help, but the EPA notes they can't remove every pollutant. Cutting pollution where it starts does a lot:

  • Run the bathroom fan during showers and the kitchen hood while cooking, both vented outdoors.
  • Keep gas appliances vented outdoors and checked, and keep carbon monoxide alarms working.
  • Keep the air conditioner's drip pan clean and its drain line flowing.

If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone outside first, then call 911 or the gas company.

Equipment that can help

Depending on the home: a deeper filter cabinet that holds a thicker filter, a whole-home dehumidifier or humidifier, or an air purifier. The indoor air quality service page lists what a visit covers.
