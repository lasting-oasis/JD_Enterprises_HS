Heat pump or furnace?

Both keep a house warm. The better fit depends on the climate, the fuel the house has and how it's set up.

How each one heats

A furnace makes heat, usually by burning natural gas, sometimes with electric heating elements. A heat pump moves heat instead of making it: in winter it pulls heat out of the outdoor air and brings it inside, and in summer it runs the other way and cools the house like an air conditioner.

Moving heat takes less energy than making it. The U.S. Department of Energy says today's heat pumps can cut the electricity used for heating by about half compared with electric resistance heat, such as electric furnaces and baseboard heaters. In its example, a heat pump rated 10.3 HSPF delivers 10,300 Btu of heat for each kilowatt-hour of electricity, where an electric heating element delivers about 3,400.

What Charlotte's winters mean

Charlotte's winters are mild. In January, the coldest month, the normal high is about 52°F and the normal low about 32°F (National Weather Service, 1991–2020 normals). That suits a heat pump: the Department of Energy describes heat pumps as an efficient alternative to furnaces and air conditioners where heating and cooling needs are moderate.

A heat pump loses some heating capacity as it gets colder outside, so most have backup heat for the coldest hours. The thermostat settings decide how often that backup runs.

Dual fuel: some of each

A dual-fuel, or hybrid, system pairs a heat pump with a gas furnace. The heat pump heats on milder days and the furnace takes over when it gets cold. The Department of Energy notes these systems are common in places like the Mid-Atlantic. In a home that already has gas and a furnace in good shape, replacing the air conditioner with a heat pump, with controls that support it, can turn the pair into a dual-fuel system.

What decides it for your home

  • Fuel: whether the house has natural gas, and whether the gas line and venting are in good order.
  • Timing: if the air conditioner is due for replacement, a heat pump replaces it and adds heating in the same unit.
  • Ducts: either system needs sealed ducts sized for the airflow. Rooms without ducts can use a ductless mini-split.
  • Energy prices: compare your own electric and gas bills, since rates change.
  • Comfort: air from a furnace feels hotter at the vents; a heat pump's air is warm and runs longer, more evenly.

Upkeep

ENERGY STAR recommends a check-up before each season. A heat pump heats and cools, so it gets a spring visit and a fall visit; a furnace gets its check in fall. The tune-up checklist covers what each visit should include.

Rebates and credits

Utility rebate programs change, so check current eligibility; the rebate help page links to the official pages. The federal 25C tax credit for heat pumps ended for equipment placed in service after December 31, 2025.
